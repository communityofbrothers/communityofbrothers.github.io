Who we are

A community of men, not just a Bible study

We are a Christian community of young men growing in faith through Scripture, prayer and fellowship, seeking to follow the example of Jesus in our lives.

Wednesday evening is when we meet — we open the Bible together, we pray, and we ask each other real questions. But the group is not only that. Across the year we eat together, get out and do things, and put our hands to work in our own community, because a faith that never leaves the room is not the one described in the New Testament.

Whether you turned to Jesus a day ago or have known him for a lifetime, we all have questions. We do not promise to have every answer, but the leadership will always give you an honest one. Whether you come from atheism, Islam, Mormonism or the Jehovah’s Witnesses, at CoB we want to know the truth, whatever it may be. So even if you do not believe, if you want to explore the truth because it matters to you, come and challenge us. We would love to explore it together.

Our mission

A brotherhood with Christ at the centre

Our mission is to create a Christian brotherhood with Christ at the centre. Jesus said, “I am the way and the truth and the life” (John 14:6). We believe that wholeheartedly, and we aspire to follow him every day.

As Christians, we recognise the challenges of daily life as believers in a fallen world. The Community of Brothers exists to be a strong network of support and encouragement for our faith in Jesus.

With God’s blessing, we aim to grow the group to serve the St Peter’s congregation and the wider Morley community, so that people can become confident in their faith in Jesus our Saviour and go on to proclaim the gospel boldly to others — the gospel that is the power of God for salvation (Romans 1:16).

Christ leads the way

What we mean by that

It does not mean we have it all worked out. It means that following Christ is supposed to show up somewhere.

In how a man works. In how he speaks about people who are not in the room. In how he treats his wife and his children. In what he does with his money, his phone and his evenings. Belief that changes nothing by Monday morning is not what the New Testament describes.

So we ask each other about it, and we expect to be asked. That is what the name means — brothers, holding each other to something.

Wednesdays

What actually happens

The group has a core leadership, whose job is to make sure what we teach stays faithful to Scripture and does not drift into error. Alongside that, we help brothers grow into leading a session themselves — taking a passage away, studying it and learning it well before sharing it with the group, with the leadership alongside them.

  1. Catch upHow the week has actually been — not how it was supposed to have been.
  2. PrayWe ask God to open his word to us before we read it.
  3. Read and work through the passage togetherWhat happens? What does it show about God? What does it show about people? What do we do about it?
  4. Discuss and applyWe make sure everyone has understood it, and that each of us takes something practical away to apply to our lives.
  5. Close in prayerWe finish by praying for each other, by name.
Beyond Wednesday

What else we get up to

Wednesday is the fixed point in the week. Around it, through the year, we put on other things — usually at a weekend, and announced as they come up. Fellowship that only ever happens sitting in a circle with Bibles open is half a thing. What builds brotherhood is time spent together doing ordinary stuff.

  • Shared meals. Eating together does more for a group of men than almost anything else, and the New Testament seems to know it.
  • Days and nights out. Walks, trips, sport, whatever somebody fancies organising. Friendship is not a programme.
  • Community work. Projects in and around Morley — practical graft for people who need a hand. Faith with its sleeves rolled up.
  • Turning up for each other. Moving house, a rough week, a hospital visit. Nobody advertises this part and everybody needs it.
Before you worry

What you will not be asked to do

These are the things that actually stop men coming, so here they are in plain words.

  • You will not be asked to pray out loud. “I’ll pray silently tonight” is a normal thing to say here and nobody thinks anything of it.
  • You will not be put on the spot. Questions are for working through together, not for testing you.
  • You do not need to know anything first. No church background, no Bible of your own, no vocabulary. We have a sheet explaining the words, because everyone needs one at the start.
  • You are not signing up to anything. Come once and see. If it is not for you, that is genuinely fine.

The Bible

The complete Scriptures, free to read. Every book has an introduction and every chapter has study notes. Every book of the New Testament now has detailed notes on every passage — context, meaning, the big idea, and where Christians read it differently — with more books being added. If you are new, start in Mark. Tap any passage heading marked “notes” to jump to its note.

Where these notes stand

The notes are ours, written for our own men. They lean Reformed in theology and are written in plain evangelical language rather than in the vocabulary of any one tradition.

Where Christians genuinely divide — the days of creation, baptism, the Lord’s Supper, election, spiritual gifts today, men and women in the church, the millennium — we say so, name the main positions fairly, and tell you which one we hold and why. Where the manuscripts are disputed, as at Mark 16:9 and John 7:53, we flag it rather than pass over it. Where a question is genuinely open, we would rather say that than sound more certain than the evidence allows.

Nothing here replaces your own church or your own reading. If you think a note is wrong, tell us and we will look at it again.

    Resources

    What we make, and why we give it away

    Everything below was written for the men in our own group, and that shapes it: plain words, no assumed knowledge, and candid about what is genuinely disputed rather than pretending every question is settled. If it is useful to you, take it. Print it, pass it on, use it in your own group. We are not selling anything.

    The thinking behind them

    Written to be used, not admired

    Most Christian material assumes you already know the vocabulary, already have the habit, and already know your way around sixty-six books. Ours does not. Each guide starts from the question a man actually asks rather than the answer we would like to give — which is why the prayer guide begins with who you are praying to, and the Bible-reading guide begins with why people stop.

    Getting hold of them

    Print it, or keep it on your phone

    Every guide downloads as a PDF, laid out to print cleanly on A4 and equally readable on a phone or laptop. Two of them — the Wednesday discussion sheet and the six-week reading plan — have proper form fields, so you can type and tick straight into them on a device instead of printing. Take them, keep them, pass them on. If you want to use any of this with your own group, you do not need our permission.

    Apologetics

    Giving a reason for the hope that is in you

    Apologetics is the work of explaining why Christians believe what they believe, and answering genuine questions honestly. The word comes from the Greek apologia — a reasoned defence, the kind you would give in a courtroom. Peter tells ordinary believers to be ready to give one, and adds that it should be offered with gentleness and respect. Both halves of that instruction matter.

    Why it matters

    Because the questions are coming anyway

    Nobody has ever been argued into the kingdom of God, and we are not trying to win debates. But a man with nothing to say when a colleague asks whether the Bible has been changed, or when someone knocks on his door with a different Jesus, tends to conclude quietly that there is no answer. There usually is one. He simply has never been shown it.

    So these exist for two reasons. The first is to steady our own men — to make sure the first serious challenge they meet is not the first time they have thought about it. The second is for anyone weighing the claims seriously, from whichever direction they come. We state other people’s positions in terms they would recognise, because an argument that only defeats a caricature is worth nothing.

    Where the true answer is that something is difficult, disputed or simply unknown, we say so. Overclaiming is how people lose their faith five years later.

    What we have

    Topic by topic

    Some are finished; others are in preparation. We would rather publish slowly and be right than quickly and be corrected.

    The Jehovah’s Witness material is a set of three, meant to be read in order

    The first is a single sheet of questions, short enough to be taken at a door and checked the same evening. The second works through those questions properly and adds more, arguing entirely from the Witness’s own Bible and interlinear rather than from ours. The third is about the organisation’s published record, and it is deliberately last — it is the hardest to read, and the case does not depend on it.

    All three give every reference in full, state the Watchtower’s own answers in the Watchtower’s own words, and list the arguments we decided against using. Nothing in them concerns anyone’s private life or motives.

    How we go about it

    Four rules we hold ourselves to

    • Quote people accurately, from their own sources. If we say a group teaches something, we show where they teach it.
    • Separate what is certain from what is arguable. Treating every point as equally strong weakens the strong ones.
    • Attack the argument, never the person. The man at your door is not your enemy, and he almost certainly believes what he is saying.
    • Say “I don’t know” when it is true. It costs nothing and it buys you the right to be believed on everything else.
    Finding a church

    Every man here needs a church, including us

    Community of Brothers is not a church and was never meant to be one. It has its own leadership, but it does not appoint elders, baptise anyone or take communion, because those belong to the church. A Wednesday night with other men is a good thing; it is not the thing the New Testament calls the church, and a man who has only this will find out eventually that something is missing.

    The simplest answer

    Come to ours

    Most of us are at the same church, and you would be welcome on a Sunday with no more ceremony than turning up on a Wednesday. Come with one of us the first time and nothing about it will be strange — you will know somebody before you walk in, which is most of what makes a first visit hard.

    Ask any of the men on a Wednesday and someone will happily meet you there and sit with you.

    If ours is not for you

    What to look for

    Ours will not suit everybody, and that is fine — distance, family, or simply where you already feel at home are all good reasons. What matters far more than which building you end up in is that you end up in one. Six things worth checking.

    • The Bible is opened and explained. The teaching should work through a passage rather than around a theme, so that you could go home and check it — which is exactly what the Bereans were commended for doing.
    • Jesus and the cross are central, not assumed. If you could sit through several weeks without hearing why his death matters, something has drifted.
    • You can find out what they believe. A church with nothing written down that you can read is a church you cannot assess.
    • People actually know each other. The test is simple: would anybody notice if you stopped coming for a month?
    • Leadership and money are accountable. Plural leadership, published accounts, and a safeguarding policy you can ask about. That is not cynicism; it is the ordinary protection of everybody involved.
    • They look outward. A church that exists only for the people already inside it has stopped doing half its job.
    And what not to worry about

    Things that matter less than you think

    Men rule churches out for reasons that have nothing to do with whether the church is any good.

    • The building. Ancient stone or a school hall. Neither tells you anything.
    • The music. Organ, band, or nothing at all. It is one of the most common reasons people pick a church and one of the least important.
    • The size. A church of thirty people who know each other beats one of three hundred who do not, and a large church with real small groups beats both.
    • What people wear. Nobody serious is checking.
    • Not understanding everything. You will not, for a while. That is what the rest of us are for, and there is a sheet explaining the vocabulary in the resources.
    A practical suggestion

    Go three times before you decide anything. One visit tells you about that morning rather than about the church — everybody has an off week and everybody can manage one good one.

    Then ask somebody there a direct question: what would you say this church is actually for? The answer, and how readily it comes, will tell you more than the service did.

    Why we keep saying this

    The risk in a group like ours

    A midweek group of men you like, who read the Bible and pray together, can quietly become somebody’s whole Christian life. It is easy to see how — it is warmer than a Sunday service, nobody is a stranger, and you can miss a week without anyone minding.

    But we cannot do for you what a church does. We are all roughly the same age and we are all men. There are no children here, no older believers who have already been through what you are about to go through, nobody to baptise you, and nobody carrying a church’s authority and responsibility to look after your soul. A church has all of that. It also has people you would never have chosen as friends, which turns out to be part of the point.

    So come on a Wednesday, and go somewhere on a Sunday. We would rather you did both — and if you can only manage one, do not let it be this one.
